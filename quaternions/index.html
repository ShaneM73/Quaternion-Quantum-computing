    Step 0 · Orientation

    Four numbers that turn the world

    i2 = j2 = k2 = ijk = −1
    Broom Bridge, Royal Canal, Dublin · 16 October 1843

    You already know that multiplying by a complex number rotates the plane. In this module you will follow William Rowan Hamilton’s ten-year hunt for the three-dimensional version, see why it needed four dimensions, and discover that the same algebra quietly runs every single-qubit gate in a quantum computer.

    What you will be able to do

    • Explain why “3D complex numbers” cannot exist with the properties Hamilton wanted.
    • Multiply quaternions by hand and explain why ij ≠ ji.
    • Compute conjugates, norms and inverses, and use the product rule (s, v)(t, w) = (st − v·w, sw + tv + v×w).
    • Rotate a 3D vector with q v q⁻¹ and explain the half angle.
    • Interpolate between orientations with slerp and say why it beats Euler angles.
    • Identify unit quaternions with SU(2), map Pauli gates to i, j, k, and read a qubit gate as a quaternion.

    Step 1 · Warm-up

    Complex numbers are rotations of the plane

    Write z = a + bi in polar form z = r eiθ = r(cos θ + i sin θ). Multiplying two complex numbers multiplies their lengths and adds their angles:

    (r eiα)(s eiβ) = rs · ei(α+β)So |zw| = |z||w|. The length of a product is the product of the lengths.

    A complex number eiα of length 1 is therefore a machine that rotates every point of the plane by α. Multiplying by i = eiπ/2 is a quarter turn, which is exactly why i² = −1: two quarter turns make a half turn, and a half turn sends 1 to −1.

    Hamilton’s first breakthrough: couples

    In 1833 Hamilton read a paper to the Royal Irish Academy (published 1837) that removed the mystery from √−1. He treated a complex number as an ordered pair of reals, an “algebraic couple”, with the rule

    (a, b)(c, d) = (ac − bd, ad + bc)

    Nothing imaginary is needed; i is just the pair (0, 1), and the rule makes (0,1)(0,1) = (−1, 0). This is how complex numbers are defined in textbooks today. It also raised an obvious question: if pairs of numbers describe rotations of the plane, can triples of numbers describe rotations of space?

    Check yourself

    Which complex number rotates the plane by 30° and doubles every length? Answer: 2eiπ/6 = √3 + i. Its length is 2 and its angle is 30°.

    Step 2 · Motivation

    Why there are no “three-dimensional complex numbers”

    Hamilton wanted numbers a + bi + cj with i² = j² = −1 that could be added, multiplied and divided, and that obeyed the law of the moduli: the length of a product equals the product of the lengths, just as for complex numbers.

    |pq| = |p| · |q|,    where |a + bi + cj|² = a² + b² + c²

    Hamilton’s own experiment

    He tried squaring a triplet. Expanding carefully, without assuming that ij equals ji:

    (a + bi + cj)² = a² − b² − c² + 2abi + 2acj + bc(ij + ji)

    For the squared triplet (a² − b² − c², 2ab, 2ac) the law of the moduli works perfectly: its length is a² + b² + c². So the leftover term must vanish, which forces ij + ji = 0, that is ji = −ij. Giving up commutativity was the first leap. But what is ij? Setting it to 0 breaks products of different triplets. Setting it equal to any combination of 1, i, j also fails. The second leap, years later, was to let ij be a new, fourth unit k.

    A number-theory proof that triples cannot work

    If a multiplication on triples with whole-number coefficients obeyed the law of the moduli, then any product of two sums of three squares would again be a sum of three squares. That is false: 3 = 1² + 1² + 1² and 5 = 2² + 1² + 0², but 15 is not a sum of three squares. Legendre showed that the numbers which fail are exactly those of the form 4a(8b + 7). With four squares the problem disappears: every whole number is a sum of four squares (Lagrange, 1770), and Euler had already found in 1748 an identity showing that a product of two sums of four squares is again one.

    The final word: Hurwitz’s theorem

    In 1898 Adolf Hurwitz proved that a real algebra with a multiplicative length (a “normed division algebra”) can only have dimension 1, 2, 4 or 8: the reals, the complex numbers, the quaternions and the octonions. Dimension 3 is impossible, no matter how clever the rules. Hamilton could not have known this in 1833; he simply kept trying.

    Check yourself

    Is 28 a sum of three squares? Answer: No. 28 = 4 · 7, and 7 = 8·0 + 7, so it has the form 4a(8b + 7). As a sum of four squares, 28 = 25 + 1 + 1 + 1.

    Step 3 · The story

    A walk along the Royal Canal

    William Rowan Hamilton (1805–1865) was a prodigy from Dublin. While still an undergraduate at Trinity College he was appointed Andrews Professor of Astronomy and Royal Astronomer of Ireland, living at Dunsink Observatory just outside the city. By the 1830s he was famous for reformulating optics and mechanics; the “Hamiltonian” of modern physics carries his name.

    From 1833 he worked off and on at multiplying triplets. Late in life he described the mood of October 1843 in a letter to his son Archibald. Every morning, he wrote, his sons would ask at breakfast:

    “Well, Papa, can you multiply triplets?”Hamilton’s reply, every time: he could only add and subtract them. (Letter to A. H. Hamilton, 1865)

    On Monday, 16 October 1843, Hamilton walked with his wife Helen along the towpath of the Royal Canal toward a meeting of the Royal Irish Academy, where he was president. Near Brougham Bridge (now Broom Bridge, in Cabra) the answer arrived all at once: use four units, and let their products follow the rule i² = j² = k² = ijk = −1. He took out a penknife and cut the formula into the stone of the bridge. He announced the discovery to the Academy that November.

    The carving has long since weathered away. A plaque on the bridge now records the moment, and since 1990 mathematicians have walked the same route from Dunsink to Broom Bridge every 16 October in the annual Hamilton Walk.

    • 1840Olinde Rodrigues publishes formulas for composing 3D rotations that are, in hindsight, quaternion multiplication.

    • 1845Arthur Cayley publishes the octonions independently; they are often called Cayley numbers.

    Check yourself

    Why is it historically fitting that quaternions were discovered by an astronomer? One answer: astronomy and navigation are full of rotations of the sphere. Hamilton wanted an algebra of space, and orientation in space is exactly what quaternions turned out to describe best.

    Step 4 · Algebra

    One line of rules, a whole multiplication table

    A quaternion is q = a + bi + cj + dk with real a, b, c, d. Addition is component by component. Multiplication distributes over addition and is associative; everything else follows from the bridge formula. Click any cell to see how its entry is derived.

    The cycle

    Go around the circle in the arrow direction and the product is positive: ij = k, jk = i, ki = j. Go against the arrows and you pick up a minus sign: ji = −k. Look familiar? It is the cross product rule x̂ × ŷ = ẑ.

    The general product

    Multiplying out (a₁ + b₁i + c₁j + d₁k)(a₂ + b₂i + c₂j + d₂k) term by term (16 terms) and collecting with the table gives

      (a₁a₂ − b₁b₂ − c₁c₂ − d₁d₂)
    + (a₁b₂ + b₁a₂ + c₁d₂ − d₁c₂) i
    + (a₁c₂ − b₁d₂ + c₁a₂ + d₁b₂) j
    + (a₁d₂ + b₁c₂ − c₁b₂ + d₁a₂) k

    Swapping the two factors flips the sign of the paired terms such as c₁d₂ − d₁c₂ and leaves everything else alone. That is the precise sense in which quaternions are noncommutative: pq − qp is twice a cross product (you will prove this in the next step).

    Check yourself

    Compute (1 + i)(1 + j) and (1 + j)(1 + i). Answer: 1 + i + j + k and 1 + i + j − k. They differ only in the sign of k.

    Step 5 · Properties

    Scalar plus vector

    Hamilton split a quaternion into a real part, which he called the scalar, and the rest, which he called the vector: q = s + v with v = bi + cj + dk. Both words are his. With this split the general product becomes

    (s + v)(t + w) = (st − v·w) + (sw + tv + v × w)The dot product and the cross product of vector analysis are the two halves of one quaternion product.

    Two consequences are worth stopping on. For two pure vectors, vw = −v·w + v×w, so vw − wv = 2v×w. And for a single unit vector u, u² = −1. Every point of the unit sphere is a square root of −1. Complex numbers have two such roots; quaternions have a whole sphere of them, and each one generates its own copy of ℂ inside ℍ.

    NotionComplex numbersQuaternions
    Conjugatez̄ = a − biq* = s − v = a − bi − cj − dk
    Norm|z|² = z z̄|q|² = q q* = a² + b² + c² + d²

    Because |pq| = |p||q|, writing it out with integer components reproduces Euler’s four-square identity. Because every nonzero q has an inverse, ℍ is a division algebra. Frobenius proved in 1877 that ℝ, ℂ and ℍ are the only finite-dimensional associative ones. The octonions keep division but lose associativity.

    Step 6 · Geometry

    The sandwich that rotates space

    Treat a 3D vector v = (x, y, z) as the pure quaternion xi + yj + zk. Take a unit axis u and an angle θ, and build the unit quaternion

    q = cos(θ/2) + u sin(θ/2)   ⟹   v ↦ q v q⁻¹ = q v q*This rotates v by θ about u, using the right-hand rule.

    Why a sandwich, and why half the angle?

    Multiplying only on one side, qv, generally produces a quaternion with a nonzero scalar part: it leaves 3D space and wanders into the fourth dimension. The second factor q* brings it back. Split v into a part parallel to u and a part perpendicular to it. The parallel part commutes with q, so the two factors cancel and it stays put. The perpendicular part anticommutes with u, so it gets rotated once by the left factor and once more by the right factor: two turns of θ/2 make θ. You can see the scalar part appear and vanish in the readout below.

    Composition and the double cover

    Rotate first by q₁, then by q₂: q₂(q₁vq₁*)q₂* = (q₂q₁)v(q₂q₁)*. Composing rotations is just multiplying quaternions, four numbers instead of nine matrix entries, and renormalizing a drifting quaternion is trivial.

    Notice in the bench that q and −q produce the same rotation, since the minus signs cancel in the sandwich. Drag θ past 360°: the block is back where it started, yet q = −1. Only at 720° does q return to +1. Unit quaternions cover the rotation group SO(3) exactly twice. Hold on to that fact; in Step 9 it becomes the physics of spin.

    Check yourself

    Which quaternion rotates 180° about the z-axis, and where does it send v = i? Answer: q = cos 90° + k sin 90° = k. Then k i k* = k i (−k) = −(ki)k = −jk = −i. The x-axis is flipped, as expected.

    Step 7 · Application

    Smooth turns and the end of gimbal lock

    Engineers often describe orientation with three Euler angles: yaw, pitch, roll. They are intuitive, but they have two flaws. First, at pitch ±90° two of the three rotation axes line up and one degree of freedom disappears. This is gimbal lock, a real hazard in mechanical gimbals; during Apollo, guidance software watched for the spacecraft’s gimbals approaching it. Second, interpolating each angle separately produces curved, wobbling paths.

    Unit quaternions live on the 3-sphere, which has no such singular points. To blend between two orientations, follow the great circle between them. This is spherical linear interpolation, introduced to computer graphics by Ken Shoemake in 1985:

    slerp(q₀, q₁; t) = q₀ (q₀* q₁)t = [sin((1−t)Ω) q₀ + sin(tΩ) q₁] / sin ΩΩ is the angle between q₀ and q₁ in ℝ⁴. If q₀·q₁ < 0, replace q₁ by −q₁ to take the shorter way round.

    Today quaternions are the standard internal representation of orientation in game engines and animation tools, spacecraft attitude control, drones, robotics, and the sensor-fusion code that tells your phone which way it is facing.

    Check yourself

    Why must a slerp implementation check the sign of q₀·q₁? Answer: because q₁ and −q₁ are the same orientation. Without the check, the blend may take the long way, turning more than 180°.

    Step 8 · History, continued

    Eclipse and return

    Hamilton spent his last twenty-two years on quaternions and believed they would become the language of physics. His Scottish disciple Peter Guthrie Tait championed them fiercely, and James Clerk Maxwell used quaternion ideas in his 1873 Treatise, where he gave the operation we now call the curl its name.

    Working physicists, however, mostly needed only half of the product at a time. In the 1880s Josiah Willard Gibbs at Yale and Oliver Heaviside in England independently split the quaternion product into the separate dot and cross products and dropped the scalar-plus-vector object altogether. In the 1890s the two camps argued in the pages of Nature and elsewhere. Lord Kelvin, no friend of the new notation, judged that quaternions had been of no real use to anyone. Vector analysis won the classroom, and quaternions faded into a curiosity.

    What survived

    • The words scalar and vector.
    • The names i, j, k for unit vectors, still printed in physics books.
    • The cross product rule i × j = k, now without the minus-one.

    What came back

    • Clifford and geometric algebras, which generalise quaternions to any dimension.
    • Spin and the Pauli matrices in quantum mechanics (1927).
    • Rotations in aerospace, graphics, robotics and phones (from the 1960s to today).

    The lesson for students is subtle. Hamilton was right that a rotation in 3D is naturally a four-number object. He was wrong that physicists would want to compute everything that way. Both views live on today, each where it fits best.

    Check yourself

    Using Step 5, explain how Gibbs’s dot and cross products sit inside one quaternion product. Answer: for pure vectors, vw = −v·w + v×w. The scalar part is minus the dot product and the vector part is the cross product.

    Step 9 · Quantum computing

    Every single-qubit gate is a quaternion

    A qubit state is a unit vector |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ in ℂ², with |α|² + |β|² = 1. Multiplying the state by a phase eiφ changes nothing measurable, so physical states form a sphere, the Bloch sphere: |0⟩ at the north pole, |1⟩ at the south, and superpositions everywhere between. Single-qubit gates are 2×2 unitary matrices. Ignoring the same global phase, we may take them to have determinant 1. That group is SU(2).

    The Pauli matrices are i, j, k in disguise

    σx = [0 1; 1 0],   σy = [0 −i; i 0],   σz = [1 0; 0 −1]They satisfy σx² = σy² = σz² = I and σxσy = iσz (and cyclically). Here i is the ordinary complex unit.

    Squares equal +I, not −1, so set 𝐢 = −iσx, 𝐣 = −iσy, 𝐤 = −iσz. Then 𝐢² = (−i)²σx² = −I, and 𝐢𝐣 = −σxσy = −iσz = 𝐤. Hamilton’s rules hold exactly. A unit quaternion a + b𝐢 + c𝐣 + d𝐤 becomes the matrix

    U = a I − i(bσx + cσy + dσz) = [ a − id   −c − ib ;   c − ib   a + id ]Every matrix in SU(2) has this form for exactly one unit quaternion. Unit quaternions and SU(2) are the same group.

    The standard rotation gate is Rn(θ) = e−iθ(n·σ)/2 = cos(θ/2) I − i sin(θ/2)(n·σ). Translate with the dictionary: it is cos(θ/2) + sin(θ/2)n, the very same quaternion that rotates 3D space by θ about n in Step 6, half angle included. A gate acts on the Bloch vector exactly as the sandwich qvq* acts on a 3D vector.

    Each gate equals its quaternion’s matrix times a global phase; for example X = i·(−iσx). The phase is invisible on the Bloch sphere.

    The double cover is real physics

    On the Bloch sphere a 360° rotation looks like doing nothing, but the quaternion, and the state vector, pick up a factor of −1. For an isolated qubit this sign is a global phase and cannot be seen. In an interferometer, where a spinning particle is compared with an unrotated copy of itself, it can. Neutron interferometry experiments in 1975 observed this sign change directly: spin-½ particles need a 720° turn to come back to themselves. That is the 2-to-1 map from unit quaternions to rotations, measured in a laboratory.

    Where quaternions show up in quantum computing

    • Gate fusion. Compilers merge long runs of single-qubit gates into one rotation. Multiplying quaternions (four reals) is cheap and numerically stable, and the result converts directly to Euler-style gate angles.
    • Gate synthesis. Fault-tolerant machines only have a finite gate set, such as Clifford + T, so arbitrary rotations must be approximated by gate sequences. Some of the best methods are number-theoretic: they search for quaternions with integer-like coordinates of a prescribed norm. This line of work goes back to Lubotzky, Phillips and Sarnak’s 1980s constructions of well-spread rotations on the sphere from integer quaternions.
    • Spin and control. Pulse sequences in NMR and in spin and superconducting qubits are routinely analysed as products of rotations, that is, as quaternion products.

    Could quantum mechanics itself be quaternionic?

    Birkhoff and von Neumann noted in 1936 that the logic of quantum theory allows state spaces over ℝ, ℂ or ℍ. Quaternionic quantum mechanics was developed seriously, first by Finkelstein, Jauch and collaborators in the early 1960s and later in a book by Stephen Adler (1995). So far no experiment demands it, and standard quantum mechanics uses complex numbers. In 2021, theory and experiments on quantum networks showed that a version of quantum theory using only real numbers makes predictions that nature violates. Complex amplitudes appear to be essential. Quaternions, meanwhile, enter through the symmetry group SU(2), not as the numbers of the theory itself.

    Check yourself

    The gate sequence H, then Z, then H is known to equal X. Verify with quaternions. Answer: with h = (i + k)/√2, the total is h k h = ½(i + k)k(i + k) = ½(ik − 1)(i + k) = ½(−j − 1)(i + k) = ½(−ji − jk − i − k) = ½(k − i − i − k) = −i. That is X up to sign, which is a global phase.

    Step 10 · Consolidate

    Review and quiz

    Key ideas

    • Unit complex numbers rotate the plane; there is no 3D analogue because normed division algebras exist only in dimensions 1, 2, 4, 8.
    • i² = j² = k² = ijk = −1, so ij = k = −ji.
    • q = s + v; the product contains both the dot and the cross product.
    • |pq| = |p||q| and q⁻¹ = q*/|q|².

    Key formulas

    • Rotation: q = cos(θ/2) + u sin(θ/2), v ↦ qvq*.
    • Compose: first q₁ then q₂ is q₂q₁.
    • q and −q give the same rotation (double cover).
    • Unit quaternions = SU(2); i ↔ −iσx, and so on. Single-qubit gates are rotations of the Bloch sphere.
