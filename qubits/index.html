Qubits on the Sphere

Qubits on the Sphere

Quantum computing, one rotation at a time · 14 steps

    Step 0 · Orientation

    Quantum computing, drawn on a sphere

    A single qubit lives on a sphere, and every operation on it is a rotation. Hamilton’s quaternions are the natural numbers for rotations. This course uses that one picture, sphere plus quaternion, to explain every core idea of quantum computing, from measurement to Grover’s algorithm and error correction.

    The Bloch sphere

    Drag to turn it · arrow keys work too

    North pole |0⟩, south pole |1⟩. On the equator: |+⟩ and |−⟩ on the x-axis, |+i⟩ and |−i⟩ on the y-axis. Every other point is a superposition.

    The dictionary you will use throughout

    Quaternion

    q = cos(θ/2) + n sin(θ/2)

    with i² = j² = k² = ijk = −1

    Qubit gate

    U = cos(θ/2) I − i sin(θ/2) (n·σ)

    an SU(2) matrix, using i ↔ −iσx, j ↔ −iσy, k ↔ −iσz

    Sphere

    r ↦ q r q*

    rotates the Bloch vector by θ about the axis n

    One quaternion, three faces. The half angle θ/2, the sign ambiguity q ≈ −q and the noncommuting i, j, k all show up as real quantum effects in the steps ahead.

    Step 1 · State

    A qubit is a point on a sphere

    A qubit state is a unit vector in ℂ²: |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ with |α|² + |β|² = 1. That is four real numbers with one constraint, so a 3-sphere, the same space as unit quaternions. Multiplying the whole state by a phase eiγ changes nothing you can ever measure. Removing that one redundant number leaves an ordinary 2-sphere, the Bloch sphere:

    |ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + eiφ sin(θ/2)|1⟩  ⟷  r = (sin θ cos φ, sin θ sin φ, cos θ)θ is the polar angle from |0⟩ and φ the azimuth. The amplitudes use θ/2, the same half angle as a rotation quaternion.

    Interactive

    Move the global phase γ: both amplitude arrows spin together, the numbers α and β change, and the point on the sphere does not move. Only the relative phase φ between the two amplitudes is physical.

    The quaternion that prepares the state

    Start at |0⟩ (the north pole). Rotate by θ about y, then by φ about z. As quaternions that is q = Rz(φ) Ry(θ) = (cos φ/2 + k sin φ/2)(cos θ/2 + j sin θ/2). The matching SU(2) matrix sends |0⟩ to e−iφ/2|ψ⟩, which is |ψ⟩ up to a global phase. The readout shows this q for your current state.

    Check yourself

    Where on the sphere is (|0⟩ − i|1⟩)/√2? Answer: θ = 90°, and eiφ = −i gives φ = 270°. That is the −y direction, the state |−i⟩.

    Step 2 · Born rule

    Measuring is projecting onto an axis

    A measurement picks an axis n through the sphere and returns one of two answers, +1 (the state n) or −1 (the state −n). The probabilities depend only on how far the Bloch vector r sticks out along that axis:

    P(+n) = (1 + r·n) / 2 = cos²(α/2)α is the angle between r and n. The half angle appears again.

    After the measurement the state collapses to the pole it reported. Measuring again along the same axis repeats the answer. A standard quantum computer measures only along z. Any other axis is reached by first rotating the state with a gate. For example, an X-basis measurement is H followed by a Z measurement, because the quaternion of H swaps the x and z axes.

    Step 3 · Single-qubit gates

    Every single-qubit gate is a rotation

    A gate is a 2×2 unitary matrix. Factor out a global phase and it lands in SU(2), which is exactly the group of unit quaternions. The sphere then turns by the sandwich r ↦ qrq*. A circuit of gates g₁, g₂, …, gn is the single quaternion q = qn ⋯ q₂ q₁, with the last gate on the left.

    Noncommutativity is physics

    Since ij = k but ji = −k, the circuits “X then Y” and “Y then X” are the same rotation with opposite quaternion signs. As matrices, XY = iZ and YX = −iZ. They differ by a global phase of −1, invisible here, but it matters once the gates are controlled by another qubit (Step 8).

    Check yourself

    Use quaternions to show HXH = Z up to phase. Answer: with h = (i + k)/√2, the product is hi = (−1 + j)/√2, so hih = ½(−1 + j)(i + k) = ½(−i − k − k + i) = −k, which is Z up to sign. Confirm it with the preset.

    Step 4 · Universality for one qubit

    Any gate is three rotations

    Every single-qubit unitary can be written as

    U = eiα Rz(β) Ry(γ) Rz(δ)   ⟺   q = (cos β/2 + k sin β/2)(cos γ/2 + j sin γ/2)(cos δ/2 + k sin δ/2)This is the Z-Y-Z Euler-angle decomposition (Nielsen & Chuang, Theorem 4.1). Real hardware often implements Rz for free in software and calibrates only one other pulse.

    Multiplying out the right-hand side gives a quaternion whose components can be read straight back as angles:

    a = cos(γ/2)cos((β+δ)/2),   d = cos(γ/2)sin((β+δ)/2),   c = sin(γ/2)cos((β−δ)/2),   b = −sin(γ/2)sin((β−δ)/2)

    Step 5 · Phase becomes probability

    How a hidden phase becomes a visible answer

    Measurement along z cannot see φ, the azimuth. Every quantum algorithm therefore ends by rotating phase information onto the z-axis. The simplest example is the circuit H → P(φ) → H, a quantum version of the Mach–Zehnder interferometer:

    1. H tips |0⟩ onto the equator at |+⟩. Both paths now have equal weight.
    2. The phase gate P(φ) = diag(1, eiφ) turns the equator by φ. Probabilities are unchanged, since the vector stays on the equator.
    3. H swaps the x and z axes. The hidden angle φ is now a tilt that measurement can see.
    P(0) = cos²(φ/2)Constructive interference at φ = 0, destructive at φ = 180°.

    Using quaternions, show that H·Z·H acts like X. Then explain why the interferometer with φ = 180° always outputs 1. Answer: P(180°) = Z, and HZH = X up to phase. So the circuit is just X applied to |0⟩.

    Step 6 · Decoherence

    Real qubits sink into the ball

    A qubit that interacts with its environment is no longer a pure state. It is described by a density matrix

    ρ = ½ (I + x σx + y σy + z σz),   |r| ≤ 1Pure states sit on the surface. Mixed states fill the inside, the Bloch ball. The centre is the maximally mixed state I/2.

    Gates still act by the quaternion sandwich: UρU† ⟷ r ↦ qrq*. Noise, however, is not a rotation. It shrinks and shifts the ball. Two time scales describe a real device:

    • T₁, energy relaxation: the qubit decays toward |0⟩, so z(t) = 1 − (1 − z₀)e−t/T₁.
    • T₂, dephasing: the equatorial part shrinks, x + iy → (x₀ + iy₀) e−t/T₂ eiΔt, while a detuning Δ makes it precess. Physics requires T₂ ≤ 2T₁.

    Relaxation and dephasing

    Interactive

    Spin echo: undoing dephasing with a half-turn

    In an ensemble, or a qubit whose frequency drifts from shot to shot, each member precesses at a slightly different rate. The spins fan out and the average Bloch vector shrinks, even though no single spin has lost anything. Erwin Hahn’s 1950 trick: at time τ apply a 180° pulse about x, the quaternion i. It maps each azimuth φ to −φ. Fast spins are now behind, slow ones ahead, and at time 2τ they all line up again.

    Step 7 · Two qubits

    When the spheres go hollow

    Two qubits have four amplitudes, a₀₀|00⟩ + a₀₁|01⟩ + a₁₀|10⟩ + a₁₁|11⟩. If the state is a product, each qubit has its own point on its own sphere. If it is entangled, neither qubit has a pure state of its own. Each sphere shows a reduced Bloch vector strictly inside the ball. For any pure two-qubit state the shrinkage measures the entanglement exactly:

    |rA|² = |rB|² = 1 − C²,   C = 2 |a₀₀a₁₁ − a₀₁a₁₀|C is the concurrence (Wootters, 1998): 0 for product states, 1 for Bell states.

    The quaternion view of two qubits

    A pair of local gates is a pair of unit quaternions (p, q). The pair acts on ℝ⁴ by v ↦ p v q*, which is how every rotation of four-dimensional space arises (SU(2) × SU(2) double-covers SO(4)). In the so-called magic basis, every two-qubit state becomes a vector in ℂ⁴ on which local gates act as these real 4D rotations. The concurrence is a quantity that those rotations cannot change. That is a geometric proof that local operations cannot create entanglement.

    Check yourself

    Apply Ry(θ) to A, then CNOT. What is C? Answer: the state is cos(θ/2)|00⟩ + sin(θ/2)|11⟩, so C = 2 cos(θ/2) sin(θ/2) = sin θ. It is maximal at θ = 90°.

    Step 8 · Controlled gates and Deutsch’s algorithm

    The target kicks the phase back to the control

    A controlled gate applies U to the target only when the control is |1⟩. If the target is an eigenvector of U, with U|u⟩ = eiλ|u⟩, the target never changes. The phase instead lands on the control’s |1⟩ branch:

    (|0⟩ + |1⟩)|u⟩  →  (|0⟩ + eiλ|1⟩)|u⟩On the control’s sphere this is a rotation by λ about z, the quaternion cos(λ/2) + k sin(λ/2). This is the engine of phase estimation, Shor’s algorithm and the quantum Fourier transform.

    Deutsch’s algorithm

    You are given a black box for a function f: {0,1} → {0,1}. It is either constant (f(0) = f(1)) or balanced (f(0) ≠ f(1)). Classically you must query it twice. David Deutsch showed in 1985 that one quantum query suffices. Put the target in |−⟩, an eigenvector of X with eigenvalue −1. The oracle |x⟩|y⟩ → |x⟩|y ⊕ f(x)⟩ then kicks the phase (−1)f(x) back onto the control.

      Check yourself

      Why must the answer qubit start in |−⟩ and not |+⟩? Answer: |+⟩ is an eigenvector of X with eigenvalue +1, so the kickback phase would always be +1 and no information reaches the query qubit.

      Step 9 · Three qubits

      Teleportation, corrected by the quaternion group

      Alice holds an unknown qubit |ψ⟩. She and Bob share a Bell pair. Bennett and colleagues showed in 1993 that Alice can transfer |ψ⟩ to Bob by sending him two classical bits. No qubit travels, and nothing goes faster than light, because Bob’s qubit is useless until the bits arrive.

      The quaternion picture makes the last step transparent. After Alice’s measurement, Bob holds |ψ⟩ turned by a half-turn about x, y, z or not at all. Those four possibilities are the quaternions 1, i, j, k. Together with their negatives they form Hamilton’s quaternion group Q₈. Alice’s two bits tell Bob which element to undo.

        Step 10 · Search

        Grover’s algorithm is a rotation

        Search N = 2ⁿ items for one marked item w. Classically this takes about N/2 looks; Lov Grover (1996) needs only about (π/4)√N. The state never leaves the plane spanned by |w⟩ and |s′⟩, the uniform superposition of the unmarked items. A plane spanned by two states behaves exactly like a qubit, so the whole algorithm runs on one effective Bloch sphere, with |s′⟩ at the north pole and |w⟩ at the south.

        • Oracle flips the sign of |w⟩. On the effective sphere that is a half-turn about z: the quaternion k.
        • Diffusion reflects about the start state |s⟩. That is a half-turn about the axis of |s⟩, the pure quaternion s = sin 2θ i + cos 2θ k, where sin θ = 1/√N.
        • Two half-turns about axes at angle 2θ make one rotation by 4θ: sk = −(cos 2θ + j sin 2θ). Hamilton described exactly this, a rotation as the product of two reflections.

        Keep iterating past the optimum and the vector overshoots the south pole: the success probability goes back down. Grover search is a rotation, and a rotation does not stop by itself. You have to know when to measure.

        For N = 4, how many iterations find w with certainty? Answer: sin θ = 1/2, so θ = 30°. One iteration takes the angle from θ to 3θ = 90°, and P = sin²(90°) = 1.

        Step 11 · Compiling for real hardware

        Approximating any rotation with H and T

        Error-corrected quantum computers can perform only a finite set of gates reliably, typically Clifford gates plus T. Yet algorithms ask for arbitrary rotations such as Rz(0.3). The solution is to search for a word in H and T whose quaternion is close to the target. H and T generate a dense subgroup of the unit quaternions, so the words get arbitrarily close.

        • Solovay–Kitaev (1990s) guarantees sequences of length O(logc(1/ε)) for accuracy ε, with c close to 4 in the standard Dawson–Nielsen version.
        • Number-theoretic synthesis. Clifford+T matrices have entries in the ring ℤ[1/√2, i] (Kliuchnikov, Maslov and Mosca, 2013). Ross and Selinger (2016) solve the resulting Diophantine equations to get z-rotations with about 3 log₂(1/ε) T gates. That is essentially optimal.

        Why does the search never need the word “HH”? Answer: H is a half-turn, so h² = −1: HH is the identity rotation. Similarly TTTTTTTT is the quaternion −1.

        Step 12 · Protecting a qubit

        Catching a continuous error with a discrete question

        Errors on a real qubit are small rotations by arbitrary angles, not neat bit flips. The three-qubit bit-flip code shows the key idea of quantum error correction. Measuring the right parities forces any small x-rotation to become either “no error” or “a full X error”, and the second can be undone exactly.

        1. Encode: α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ → α|000⟩ + β|111⟩ using two CNOTs. This is not three copies, which the no-cloning theorem forbids.
        2. Error: one qubit is rotated by Rx(ε), the quaternion cos(ε/2) + i sin(ε/2).
        3. Syndrome: measure the parities Z₀Z₁ and Z₁Z₂. They reveal where an error is but nothing about α or β.
        4. Correct: apply X, the quaternion i, to the qubit the syndrome points to.

        Three-qubit code

        Interactive
          Weight in each syndrome sector

          Notice the three small spheres after encoding. Each physical qubit sits on the z-axis inside its ball, so no single qubit holds the phase φ. The information lives in the correlations, which is exactly why a local error cannot destroy it. Shor’s 1995 nine-qubit code adds the phase-flip version to protect against all errors, and today’s surface codes scale the same idea up.

          Check yourself

          With an error angle ε on one qubit, what is the chance the syndrome reports “no error”? Answer: cos²(ε/2). Either way the corrected output is perfect.

          Step 13 · Consolidate

          Review and quiz

          On the sphere

          • State ↔ point; mixed state ↔ point inside the ball.
          • Gate ↔ rotation; measurement ↔ projection onto an axis, P = (1 + r·n)/2.
          • Noise shrinks the ball; entanglement empties each qubit’s sphere.
          • Grover’s algorithm is a rotation by 4θ on an effective sphere.

          In quaternions

          • Gate = unit quaternion; circuit = product, last gate on the left.
          • X, Y, Z = i, j, k; H = (i+k)/√2; Rn(θ) = cos θ/2 + n sin θ/2.
          • q and −q are the same rotation. The sign is a global phase until a gate is controlled.
          • Teleportation corrections are the quaternion group {±1, ±i, ±j, ±k}.
